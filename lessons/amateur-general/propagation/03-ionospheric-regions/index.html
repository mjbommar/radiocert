The Ionospheric Regions, Scatter, and NVIS

Name the ionospheric regions and what each does, explain why the low bands are local by day, and recognise HF scatter and NVIS when you hear them.

11 min readG3CPropagationcoreDraft

The four regions

Which ionospheric region is closest to the surface of Earth?

The D region.

From the bottom up: D, E, F1, F2. They are not sharply bounded layers but regions of differing ionisation, and their behaviour differs in a way that explains almost everything about HF.

The D region only absorbs

Which ionospheric region is the most absorbent of signals below 10 MHz during daylight hours?

The D region.

The D region refracts essentially nothing useful. What it does is absorb, and it absorbs low frequencies most.

Why is long-distance communication on the 40-, 60-, 80-, and 160-meter bands more difficult during the day?

The D region absorbs signals at these frequencies during daylight hours.

That is the single most useful propagation fact at General level. The low bands are local by day and worldwide by night, and the reason is that the D region forms in sunlight and disappears after dark.

It is why 80 metre nets run in the evening, why 160 metres is a winter-night band, and why an 80 metre contact at noon reaches the next county and at midnight reaches another continent — on the same antenna, at the same power.

The F2 region carries the distance

Why is skip propagation via the F2 region longer than that via the other ionospheric regions?

Because it is the highest.

Pure geometry: reflect off a higher point and the wave travels further before it comes back down. F2 at about 250–400 km gives roughly a 2,500-mile hop; the E region at 90–150 km gives about 1,200.

F2 also persists overnight, while F1 merges into it and E weakens. The night-time high-band DX you occasionally hear is F2 hanging on.

Critical frequency and critical angle

Two terms that sound similar and mean different things.

What is meant by the term “critical frequency” at a given incidence angle?

The highest frequency which is refracted back to Earth.

A property of the ionosphere at that moment, for that angle. Above it, signals at that angle escape.

What does the term “critical angle” mean, as applied to radio wave propagation?

The highest takeoff angle that will return a radio wave to Earth under specific ionospheric conditions.

A property of the geometry, for a given frequency. Radiate steeper than the critical angle and the wave punches through.

The two are two views of the same threshold: for any frequency there is a steepest angle that comes back, and for any angle there is a highest frequency that comes back.

This is why antenna takeoff angle matters as much as gain on HF. A low antenna radiates at high angles — good for short hops, useless for DX. A high antenna, or one over good ground, radiates at low angles and reaches further.

HF scatter

Between the ground wave’s reach and the first skip landing point there is a skip zone — a ring where, in theory, nothing is heard. In practice something usually is.

What type of propagation allows signals to be heard in the transmitting station’s skip zone?

Scatter.

Small amounts of energy are scattered out of the main path by irregularities in the ionosphere and by the ground, and some of it lands in the skip zone.

Three characteristics, all asked:

What is a characteristic of HF scatter?

Signals have a fluttering sound.

What makes HF scatter signals often sound distorted?

Energy is scattered into the skip zone through several different paths.

Why are HF scatter signals in the skip zone usually weak?

Only a small part of the signal energy is scattered into the skip zone.

Weak, fluttering, and distorted — because it is a small fraction of the energy arriving by several routes at once. Recognisable immediately once described.

NVIS

What is near vertical incidence skywave (NVIS) propagation?

Short distance MF or HF propagation at high elevation angles.

Deliberately radiating almost straight up, so the signal comes back down within a few hundred miles — filling exactly the gap that ground wave cannot reach and skip overshoots.

NVIS wants the opposite of a DX antenna: a low horizontal wire, a quarter wavelength or less above ground, on a low band. It is the standard technique for regional emergency communications, where the traffic is 50 to 300 miles away and a repeater network may be down.

The takeoff-angle discussion above is why it works: a low antenna’s high-angle radiation, usually a defect, is the whole point here.

Check yourself

  1. Why does an 80 metre net meet in the evening rather than at lunchtime?
  2. You hear a weak, fluttering, distorted signal from a station 400 miles away while the band is otherwise carrying 2,000-mile contacts. What is it?
  3. You need reliable contact with stations 150 miles away when repeaters are down. What propagation mode and what antenna?
Answers
  1. The D region absorbs the low bands during daylight and disappears after dark, so 80 metres goes from local to long-distance at sunset.
  2. HF scatter into the skip zone — weak because only a small part of the energy scatters, fluttering and distorted because it arrives by several paths.
  3. NVIS — high-angle radiation on a low band, from a low horizontal antenna a quarter wavelength or less above ground.
