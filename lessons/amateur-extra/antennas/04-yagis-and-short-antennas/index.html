Yagis, Parabolics, and Electrically Short Antennas

Say what a Yagi's parasitic elements actually do, compute a parabolic's gain change with diameter, and place a loading coil where it costs least.

What parasitic elements do

What is the purpose of making a Yagi’s parasitic elements either longer or shorter than resonance?

Control of phase shift

That is the mechanism, and it is worth having precisely. A parasitic element re-radiates what it absorbs, with a phase that depends on its reactance:

  • Longer than resonant → inductive → lags → acts as a reflector.
  • Shorter than resonant → capacitive → leads → acts as a director.

The lengths are not arbitrary and they are not about size. They set phase, and phase decides which direction the re-radiated energy reinforces.

Approximately how long is a Yagi’s driven element?

1/2 wavelength.

Why do most two-element Yagis with normal spacing have a reflector instead of a director?

Higher gain.

At the spacings a two-element Yagi actually uses, a reflector gives more forward gain than a director does. A director wins on front-to-back ratio, and gain usually matters more — the gain-versus-pattern trade from the General track, resolved.

Circular polarisation from linear antennas

How can two linearly polarized Yagi antennas be used to produce circular polarization?

Arrange two Yagis on the same axis and perpendicular to each other with the driven elements at the same point on the boom and fed 90 degrees out of phase

Two perpendicular linear antennas fed in quadrature produce a rotating field. That matters for satellite work, where the spacecraft’s orientation is unknown and changing — circular polarisation avoids the deep fades that spin fading produces on a linear antenna.

Parabolic reflectors

How much does the gain of an ideal parabolic reflector antenna increase when the operating frequency is doubled?

6 dB

Gain scales with area, and doubling the diameter quadruples the area. Four times the power is 6 dB.

The same relationship read the other way: 3 dB of extra gain needs a 1.4× larger dish. Dish gain gets expensive fast, which is why microwave work uses the highest frequency it can — gain for a given diameter rises with frequency too.

Electrically short antennas

An antenna much shorter than a quarter wavelength is capacitively reactive. Making it resonant means cancelling that reactance.

What is the function of a loading coil in an electrically short antenna?

To resonate the antenna by cancelling the capacitive reactance.

That is all resonance means here. And the placement question is the interesting one:

What is the most efficient location for a loading coil on an electrically short whip?

Near the center of the vertical radiator

What is an advantage of top loading an electrically short HF vertical antenna?

Improved radiation efficiency.

Both answers point the same way, and the reason is current distribution. Radiation is proportional to current, and current is highest at the base of a monopole and falls toward the top. A loading coil placed at the base sits where the current is greatest and therefore dissipates the most. Moving it up — centre, or better still top — puts it where the current is lower, so it wastes less.

Top loading with a capacitance hat, from the General mobile lesson, does the same job with almost no coil at all.

Why should antenna loading coils have a high ratio of reactance to resistance?

To maximize efficiency.

Reactance over resistance is Q, from the electrical principles lesson. A high-Q coil provides the needed reactance with less loss resistance, so more of the power radiates instead of warming the coil.

What short antennas cost

Three consequences, all asked, and all the same physics.

How does radiation resistance of a base-fed whip antenna change below its resonant frequency?

Radiation resistance decreases.

Shorter electrically means less radiation resistance — which means the same fixed loss resistance becomes a larger share, so efficiency falls. This is the mobile HF problem quantified.

What happens to SWR bandwidth when one or more loading coils are used to resonate an electrically short antenna?

It is decreased.

What happens as the Q of an antenna increases?

SWR bandwidth decreases.

Loading raises Q, and BW = f₀/Q. A heavily loaded antenna is a high-Q circuit and therefore narrow — the same relationship as the matching network in the electrical principles lesson, and the reason a screwdriver antenna needs a motor.

The chain worth holding: short antenna → loading needed → high Q → narrow bandwidth and low radiation resistance → low efficiency. One cause, four consequences, and no way to escape it other than making the antenna longer.

Check yourself

  1. A Yagi’s parasitic element is cut 5% longer than the driven element. Reflector or director, and why?
  2. Your 3 metre dish gives 30 dBi. What would a 6 metre dish give?
  3. You are fitting a loading coil to a short 40 metre vertical. Base, centre, or top?
Answers
  1. Reflector. Longer than resonant is inductive, which lags, and a lagging re-radiation reinforces forward — putting the element behind the driven one.
  2. Doubling the diameter adds 6 dB: about 36 dBi.
  3. As high as practical — centre is better than base, top better still, because current is lower there and the coil dissipates less.
