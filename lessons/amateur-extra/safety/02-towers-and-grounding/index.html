Tower Climbing and Grounding

The two physical hazards that actually kill amateurs — falling and lightning — and the specific practices that prevent each.

8 min readE0ASafety & RF ExposurecoreDraft

Climbing

Three questions, one practice, and it is the practice that matters more than the answers.

What is meant by “100% tie-off” regarding tower safety?

At least one lanyard attached to the tower at all times.

At all times — including while moving. A fall-arrest harness with two lanyards makes this possible: you attach the second before releasing the first, so you are never unattached even for the moment it takes to pass an obstruction.

To what should lanyards be attached while climbing?

Tower legs.

The legs are the structural members. Rungs are for standing on, guy brackets are attachment hardware for something else, and a mast is not part of the tower’s structure. None of them is rated to arrest a fall.

Where should a shock-absorbing lanyard be attached to a tower when working above ground?

Above the climber’s head level.

Above head level, which minimises the distance you fall before the lanyard takes the load. Attach at waist height and you fall twice the lanyard’s length before it catches — and the peak force rises with the drop, which is what the shock absorber is there to manage. Give it less to absorb.

The three together: always attached, to a tower leg, above your head.

Grounding

What is the primary function of an external earth connection or ground rod?

Lightning charge dissipation.

Note what it is not for. It is not the safety ground in your mains wiring, which comes back through the service panel. It is not an RF ground for antenna performance. Its primary job is giving a lightning charge a low-impedance path into the earth before it goes through your equipment — or you.

The distinction matters because the three grounds are often confused and served by different conductors:

GroundPurposePath
Lightningdissipate a strike into earthshort, straight, heavy conductor to a rod
Mains safetytrip a breaker on a faultback through the service panel
RFprovide a counterpoise or referenceradials, or the station bonding system

All station ground rods must be bonded together and to the mains service ground — separate, unbonded rods can sit at very different potentials during a strike, and the difference appears across your equipment.

Check yourself

  1. You need to pass a guy bracket while climbing. How do you stay 100% tied off?
  2. Why attach the lanyard above your head rather than at your waist?
  3. You drive a new ground rod for the tower, well away from the house. What must you do next?
Answers
  1. Attach the second lanyard above the obstruction first, then release the first. Two lanyards are what make continuous attachment possible.
  2. It minimises the fall distance before the lanyard loads, and therefore the peak force the shock absorber must dissipate.
  3. Bond it to the other station ground rods and to the mains service ground. Unbonded rods can differ greatly in potential during a strike, and that difference appears across your equipment.
