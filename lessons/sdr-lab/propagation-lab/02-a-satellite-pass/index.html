Doppler and the Shape of a Satellite Pass

Record one satellite pass and read the orbit off the waterfall — the Doppler S curve, the closest approach, and the fading that argues for circular polarisation.

11 min readPropagationcoreDraft

Hardware

  • RTL-SDR and any VHF/UHF antennaWithout it: Several public SDRs cover 137 MHz and can be driven from a browser; a recorded IQ file of a pass works equally well for the analysis.
  • Tracking software: Gpredict, or any satellite pass appWithout it: Pass times are published on n2yo.com and similar sites.

One pass, one recording

A low Earth orbit satellite is visible for eight to fifteen minutes. In that time it moves fast enough that its Doppler shift is obvious on the display — no measurement equipment required, just a wide enough view.

The exercise: record the whole pass without touching the tuning, then look at what you captured.

Setting up

  1. Pick a target. The NOAA weather satellites at 137 MHz are ideal — strong, continuous, and predictable. The ISS packet downlink at 145.825 MHz works too.
  2. Get a pass prediction. Any tracking app; a pass reaching more than 40° elevation gives the clearest result.
  3. Tune to the satellite’s nominal frequency and set a span of at least 20 kHz so the whole Doppler excursion stays on screen.
  4. Do not touch the dial for the entire pass. The point is to see the signal move.

What you will see

The waterfall shows a signal that starts high in frequency, sweeps downward through the nominal frequency, and ends low. An S-shaped curve lying on its side.

Three things to read off it:

The total excursion. At 137 MHz a LEO satellite shifts about ±3 kHz — roughly 6 kHz peak to peak. The shift scales with frequency, so the same orbit at 435 MHz gives about ±10 kHz, which is why UHF satellite work needs continuous tuning and VHF often does not.

The steepest point is closest approach. Doppler shift depends on the rate of change of range. It is largest when the satellite is coming straight at you or straight away, and passes through zero at the moment of closest approach — where the curve crosses the nominal frequency. That crossing is TCA, and you can read it off your recording to within a few seconds.

The slope tells you the pass geometry. A high overhead pass produces a sharp, almost vertical transition through zero — the range changes fast. A low pass on the horizon produces a gentle slope. You can distinguish a 10° pass from an 80° one from the waterfall alone.

The fading

Look closely at the signal’s amplitude during the pass. It will not be steady. Expect regular deep fades, several per minute.

Two causes, both from the satellites lesson:

  • Spin modulation — the satellite is rotating, and its linearly polarised antenna sweeps past your polarisation and back.
  • Faraday rotation — the ionosphere rotates the polarisation of the signal passing through it, by an amount that varies with the path.

Both make a linearly polarised receiving antenna fade deeply and unpredictably. This is the observation that justifies a circularly polarised antenna, and having watched a NOAA image get shredded by polarisation fading you will not forget why.

Turning it into a measurement

With the recording in hand:

  1. Measure the peak upward and peak downward offsets. Halve the difference — that is the one-way Doppler magnitude.
  2. Compute the satellite’s line-of-sight velocity at the start of the pass:

Check yourself

  1. Where in the pass is the Doppler shift zero?
  2. You observe a total Doppler excursion of 20 kHz peak to peak on a 435 MHz satellite. Is that plausible?
  3. Your recording shows the signal fading deeply about every eight seconds. What is that, and what fixes it?
Answers
  1. At closest approach (TCA), where the range stops decreasing and starts increasing, so the rate of change of range is momentarily zero.
  2. Yes — about ±10 kHz at 435 MHz, since Doppler shift scales with frequency and a LEO gives roughly ±3 kHz at 137 MHz.
  3. Spin modulation, and possibly Faraday rotation. A circularly polarised antenna is insensitive to the orientation and largely removes both.
