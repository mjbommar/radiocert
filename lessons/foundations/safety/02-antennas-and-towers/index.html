Antennas, Towers, and Power Lines

Put up an antenna without contacting a power line, and know the tower rules that exist because someone died learning them.

8 min readT0BSafety & RF ExposureintroDraft

The rule that matters most

Stay clear of overhead electrical lines. Every other item in this lesson is secondary to it.

The minimum safe distance from a power line when installing an antenna is enough that if the antenna falls, no part of it can come closer than ten feet to the power wires. Not the distance while you are holding it upright — the distance if it comes down, in the direction it would come down.

This is why you never attach an antenna to a utility pole: the antenna could contact high-voltage power lines, and a utility pole is a structure built to carry exactly what you must not touch.

A vertical antenna and a length of wire are conductive, long, and awkward. The common accident is not dramatic; it is someone walking a mast upright, losing control of it, and having it fall across a service drop.

Climbing

The pool asks under what circumstances it is safe to climb a tower without a helper or observer. The answer is never.

Not “when it is short”. Not “when you are experienced”. Never.

What is required when climbing:

  • A carefully inspected climbing harness, and a helper or observer,
  • the harness clipped to the tower before releasing your grip, and
  • not climbing above the top guy attachment on a guyed tower.

Crank-up towers must not be climbed unless they are retracted, or fully supported by blocks. The cable can fail; the sections can telescope down; both have killed people.

Guy wires and turnbuckles

A safety wire through a turnbuckle prevents it loosening from vibration. Guy tension is set once; wind then works at it for years, and a turnbuckle that backs off slackens a guy and unbalances the whole system.

Grounding a tower

Two facts, both counterintuitive:

Separate eight-foot ground rods for each tower leg, all bonded together. Not one rod for the tower — one per leg, then bonded, and then bonded to the station’s ground system. Surge current wants the shortest path to earth from wherever it lands.

Avoid sharp bends in grounding conductors. A lightning surge is a very fast transient, and a sharp bend presents inductance to it. The surge will jump the bend rather than follow it, which puts the energy somewhere you did not choose. Keep runs short and direct, with gentle curves.

Local electrical codes establish grounding requirements for a tower or antenna. Not the FCC, not the ARRL — the code where you live, and it is enforceable.

A pre-installation checklist

  1. Trace the path of the antenna if it falls, in every direction. Ten feet from any power line, in all of them.
  2. Never attach to a utility pole.
  3. Harness inspected; helper present; clip on before letting go.
  4. Crank-up retracted or blocked before anyone climbs.
  5. Safety wires through turnbuckles.
  6. Ground rod per leg, all bonded, short direct runs, no sharp bends.
  7. Check the local code before, not after.

Check yourself

  1. A 30-foot mast is going up 25 feet from a service drop. Acceptable?
  2. Your neighbour offers to spot you from the ground while you climb. Does that satisfy the requirement?
  3. Why does a sharp bend matter in a lightning ground conductor but not in a DC power lead?
Answers
  1. Only if, when it falls, no part reaches within ten feet of the lines. A 30-foot mast 25 feet away does not clear that — it can reach the line.
  2. Yes, a helper or observer is required and a competent one satisfies it. The harness, its inspection, and clipping on before releasing your grip are all still required.
  3. A lightning surge is a fast transient, so conductor inductance matters and a sharp bend is inductance the surge will jump. DC sees only resistance, and a bend adds none.
