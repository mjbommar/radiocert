Beyond the Horizon

Name the propagation modes that occasionally carry VHF hundreds or thousands of miles, and say which band and which conditions each needs.

8:22
transcript
10 min readT3CPropagationcoreDraft

The normal case, and why it is the normal case

Why are simplex UHF signals rarely heard beyond their radio horizon?

UHF signals are usually not propagated by the ionosphere.

The ionosphere reflects HF and stops reflecting somewhere in the VHF range. Above that, signals go straight out into space instead of bending back, so VHF and UHF operation is fundamentally line of sight.

What is a characteristic of HF communication compared with communications on VHF and higher frequencies?

Long-distance ionospheric propagation is far more common on HF.

That single difference is why Technician privileges feel local and General privileges feel global. It is also why every exception below is worth knowing — they are the occasions when VHF stops behaving like VHF.

The radio horizon is further than the visual one

Why is the radio horizon for VHF and UHF signals more distant than the visual horizon?

The atmosphere refracts radio waves slightly.

Air density falls with altitude, and the resulting gradient bends radio waves gently downward — enough to add roughly 15% to the geometric horizon. So “line of sight” is a little more generous than the phrase suggests, and antenna height buys range faster than power does.

The exceptions, and what each needs

Sporadic E

Which of the following types of propagation is most commonly associated with occasional strong signals on the 10-, 6-, and 2-meter bands from beyond the radio horizon?

Sporadic E

Patches of intensely ionised E region form, usually in early summer, and reflect VHF for a few hundred to a couple of thousand miles. It appears without warning, lasts minutes to hours, and is the reason 6 metres is called the Magic Band.

For a Technician this is the single most rewarding propagation mode to watch for: it works on 6 and 2 metres, where you hold full privileges.

Tropospheric ducting

What type of propagation is responsible for allowing over-the-horizon VHF and UHF communications to ranges of approximately 300 miles on a regular basis?

Tropospheric ducting

What causes tropospheric ducting?

Temperature inversions in the atmosphere.

A warm layer over a cool one forms a waveguide in the lower atmosphere, and VHF or UHF signals travel inside it for hundreds of miles. Unlike sporadic E, ducting often persists for hours or days, and it favours the higher bands — 70 centimetres ducts beautifully.

Common on calm high-pressure nights and over water. If a distant repeater suddenly appears at full strength on a still evening, this is why.

Meteor scatter

What band is best suited for communicating via meteor scatter?

6 meters.

Meteors leave brief trails of ionisation that reflect VHF for a fraction of a second to a few seconds. Six metres is the sweet spot: high enough to escape HF noise, low enough that the trails reflect well.

The bursts are too short for conversation, which is why digital modes built for very short exchanges dominate this mode.

Auroral backscatter

What is one characteristic of VHF signals received via auroral backscatter?

They are distorted, with a characteristic raspy sound.

Signals bounced off the disturbed auroral zone are Doppler-smeared by the moving ionisation, so voice becomes barely intelligible and CW acquires a rough buzz. Distinctive enough that you will recognise it the first time you hear it.

F region

Which of the following bands may provide long-distance communications via the ionosphere’s F region during the peak of the sunspot cycle?

6 and 10 meters

The F region is the workhorse of HF long-distance propagation, and at solar maximum it reaches high enough in frequency to include 10 metres and occasionally 6. This is the mode that lets a Technician with 10 metre privileges work other continents.

What is generally the best time for long-distance 10-meter band propagation via the F region?

From dawn to shortly after sunset during periods of high sunspot activity.

Two conditions, both necessary: daylight, because the F region needs solar ionisation, and high sunspot activity, because that determines how high in frequency the region will reflect at all. In a solar minimum, 10 metres is quiet regardless of the hour.

Check yourself

  1. It is a still, clear evening under high pressure and a 70 cm repeater 200 miles away is full quieting. What mode?
  2. Which propagation mode would you plan a 6 metre contact around at solar maximum, in the middle of the day?
  3. Why is a UHF simplex contact beyond the horizon unusual?
Answers
  1. Tropospheric ducting — a temperature inversion, favouring UHF, lasting hours.
  2. F region propagation: dawn to shortly after sunset, during high sunspot activity.
  3. UHF signals are usually not propagated by the ionosphere, so they leave rather than bend back.
