Identification, Repeaters, and Third Parties

Identify correctly in every case the rules cover, and know who answers when a repeater carries something it should not.

9 min readT1FRules & RegulationsintroDraft

The identification rule, in full

At least every 10 minutes during, and at the end of, a communication.

Ten minutes, not fifteen — the GMRS interval is fifteen, and holding both licences is exactly how people get them confused. Amateur is ten.

Four details the pool tests separately:

Language. For a phone emission, identify in English. Other languages are permitted for the conversation; the identification itself must be English.

Method. For a station transmitting phone signals, send the call sign using a CW or phone emission. You may speak it or send it in Morse; a digital-mode identifier is not sufficient on a phone transmission.

Tactical call signs do not replace your call sign. Using “Race Headquarters” or “Net Control” is permitted and useful, and you still identify with your FCC-assigned call sign at least every 10 minutes during and at the end of the communication.

Self-assigned indicators. Appending something to your call sign — /M for mobile, /QRP, a location — is acceptable, and the pool answers “all these choices are correct”. The requirement is only that the indicator not conflict with a prefix assigned to another country and not be misleading.

Repeaters

What type of amateur station simultaneously retransmits the signal of another amateur station on a different channel or channels?

Repeater station.

Simultaneously and on a different frequency — that pair distinguishes a repeater from a store-and-forward digipeater and from a simple relay.

The accountability question is the one worth internalising:

Who is accountable if a repeater inadvertently retransmits communications that violate the FCC rules?

The control operator of the originating station.

The person who said it, not the repeater owner. This is deliberate and it is fair: a repeater cannot evaluate content in real time, so responsibility rests with whoever put the transmission on the air. It also means “the repeater carried it” is not a defence.

Third-party communications

What is the definition of third-party communications?

A message from a control operator to another amateur station control operator on behalf of another person.

You are the first party, the station you are working is the second, and anyone on whose behalf the message travels is the third. Letting a non-licensed visitor speak into your microphone is third-party traffic, with you as control operator throughout.

Domestically this is straightforward. Internationally it is not:

Which of the following restrictions apply when a non-licensed person speaks to a foreign amateur radio station via a station under the control of an FCC-licensed amateur radio operator?

The foreign station must be in a country with which the U.S. has a third-party agreement

Not every country has one. The list is published and worth checking before handing the microphone to a visitor for an international contact.

Club stations

Which of the following is a requirement for the issuance of a club station license grant?

The club must have at least four members

Four. Plus a trustee who holds an amateur licence and takes responsibility for the station. Club licences are how a group holds a call sign that outlives any individual member.

FCC inspection

When must the station licensee make the station and the station records available for inspection?

At any time upon request by an FCC representative.

Any time, on request. No notice period, no warrant requirement in the rule. In practice inspections are rare and usually follow an interference complaint, but the obligation is unconditional and it is part of what the licence costs.

Check yourself

  1. You have been running a net as “Net Control” for 25 minutes. What have you owed, and when?
  2. A visitor to your station says something prohibited, and it goes out through a repeater. Who is accountable?
  3. Your unlicensed neighbour wants to greet an amateur in another country from your station. What must you check first?
Answers
  1. Your FCC-assigned call sign, at least every 10 minutes and at the end. A tactical call sign never substitutes for it.
  2. You — the control operator of the originating station. The repeater’s owner is not accountable for what it inadvertently carries.
  3. That the other country has a third-party agreement with the United States.
